Voyager 2

Launched sixteen days before its twin, on a once-in-176-years planetary alignment. The only machine to have seen Uranus and Neptune. Forty-nine years on, it is one of two working instruments humanity has in interstellar space, and no approved successor is being built.

The Neptune encounter is what fixed this heading. An earlier candidate route would have carried Voyager 2 within 0.8 light-years of Sirius — but taking it meant skipping a close pass over Triton. Mission planners chose the science. The polar-crown trajectory they flew instead bent the probe steeply south, out of the plane every other spacecraft travels in.

Power ledger

Every watt is spent once

RTG OUTPUT · 470 W AT LAUNCH → ≈— W NOW

Three thermoelectric generators convert the heat of decaying plutonium-238 into electricity. The fuel has an 87.7-year half life; the thermocouples degrade faster than the fuel does. The spacecraft loses roughly four watts a year, and there is no way to add any back. Since 2024 the mission has been paying for science by switching science off — instrument by instrument, each command treated as permanent.

Three of eleven experiment channels are still returning data. When the cosmic ray subsystem goes dark, Voyager 2 will be down to the magnetometer and the plasma wave subsystem — the same pair its twin is already reduced to. A July 2026 power reconfiguration bought the mission roughly another year across a 39-hour command-and-response delay.

Voyager 2 is an outer-planets probe that happens to have left the solar system. It was never designed for this. Nearest concrete successor concepts: the JHU-APL Interstellar Probe study (1,000 AU in about 50 years via a solar Oberth maneuver) and Breakthrough Starshot (gram-scale laser sails at 0.2c). Neither is a funded mission.

What is ahead

Two instruments, then silence

Voyager 2 is not aimed at anything. It will fall through the galaxy for as long as the galaxy lasts, carrying a gold-plated copper record that stays playable for something on the order of a billion years — far longer than the spacecraft will be able to say anything about itself.
