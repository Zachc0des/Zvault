A field guide to artificial intelligence

Five things people mean when they say "AI," in the order you'll usually meet them — what each one actually is, where you've already run into it, and one small thing you can go do today to see it for yourself.

each ring sits inside the one before it — an LLM is a kind of deep learning, which is a kind of machine learning, which is a kind of AI. agents and AGI are drawn separately because they aren't just narrower categories.

Think of this less like a technical manual and more like a naturalist's notebook. Every entry below follows the same three questions: what is this thing, plainly, without jargon? Where have you likely already crossed paths with it? And what's the smallest possible thing you could go do, right now, with no prior experience, to actually see it in action rather than just read about it?

specimen no. 1 · the broadest one

Machine learning

the parent category everything else here belongs to

what it is

Software that gets better at a task by studying examples, instead of being handed a list of rules by a programmer. Rather than writing "if the email contains the phrase 'act now', mark it spam," you show the software thousands of real spam and real non-spam emails, and it works out the pattern on its own.

spotted in the wild

The spam filter quietly cleaning your inbox. Netflix or Spotify guessing what you'll want next, based on what you and people like you watched or played before. Your bank flagging a card charge made on the other side of the world.

try this today

Open Google's Teachable Machine in a browser (free, no account needed). Show it your webcam holding two different objects — a mug, then a book — and watch it learn to tell them apart in real time. That ten-minute exercise is machine learning end to end.

specimen no. 2 · found inside specimen no. 1

Deep learning

a specific way of doing machine learning

what it is

A style of machine learning built from stacked layers loosely inspired by neurons in a brain. Each layer notices slightly more complicated patterns than the layer before it — the first layer might notice edges and colors, the next shapes, the next whole objects like "a face" or "a stop sign."

spotted in the wild

The face unlock on your phone. A self-driving car's cameras picking out pedestrians and lane markings. Siri or Google Assistant turning your voice into words on a screen.

try this today

Open your phone's photo app and search "dog" or "beach," even for photos you never tagged. That search works because a deep learning model already looked at every picture and recognized what's in it. Try a tricky photo — a friend's dog wearing a costume — and notice where it gets confused; the mistakes are as informative as the hits.

specimen no. 3 · the innermost ring

Large language models

a deep learning model trained on text

what it is

A deep learning model trained on an enormous pile of text, whose whole job is predicting a plausible next word (or word-fragment). That sounds trivial, but string enough good next-word guesses together and you get something that can hold a conversation, answer questions, translate, and write.

spotted in the wild

Claude and ChatGPT. The autocomplete that finishes your sentences in Gmail. The newer, noticeably more fluent translations from Google Translate or DeepL compared to a decade ago.

try this today

Open a chat with an LLM — this one included — and ask it to explain something you already understand well. Then check where it's wrong. LLMs sound equally confident whether they're right or not, and finding a "confidently wrong" moment is the fastest way to build real intuition for how they work.

specimen no. 4 · built on top of no. 3

AI agents (agentic AI)

an LLM given a goal, tools, and repeated turns

what it is

An LLM that's been given tools and permission to take more than one step on its own — searching, running code, checking results, adjusting — instead of just producing a single chat reply. The "agentic" part is that loop: plan, act, look at what actually happened, correct course, repeat until the goal is met.

spotted in the wild

A coding assistant that reads your project, writes a fix, runs the tests, and rewrites the fix if the tests fail. A travel-planning agent that checks prices across sites and assembles a full itinerary. A customer service bot that doesn't just answer your question but actually processes the refund.

try this today

Give an AI assistant with tool access a task with a checkable, multi-step outcome — "find three flights to Chicago next weekend under $300 and summarize them" — rather than a single question. Pay attention to the work happening between your request and the final answer; that in-between work is the agentic part.

specimen no. 5 · unconfirmed sighting

AGI

artificial general intelligence — a target, not a product

what it is

A hypothetical AI that could learn and reason across any domain at least as well as a person can — the way one human can learn to cook, do their taxes, and play chess without being separately "trained" for each. Every specimen above this one is narrow: good at what it was built for, unreliable outside it. AGI doesn't exist yet. It's a target researchers are working toward, not something you can currently open an app and try.

spotted in the wild

Nowhere confirmed. The closest cultural reference points are fiction — HAL 9000, Data from Star Trek — rather than any deployed system. This is the one entry in the guide that no one has actually caught.

try this today

Stay skeptical of anything marketed as "AGI" right now. A useful habit instead: notice where today's AI actually breaks — ask an LLM to track a long chess game move by move, or to do reliable multi-digit long division — and you're looking directly at the gap between narrow AI and general intelligence.

for the total beginner

A four-stop trail, in order

If you only have an afternoon, do these four things in this order. Each one takes under twenty minutes and needs nothing installed.

Play before you study

Open a chat with an LLM with no task in mind — just curiosity. Ask it to explain something, write something odd, or disagree with you. You're building a feel for how it talks before you worry about how it works.

Watch a model learn, live

Use Google's Teachable Machine to train a tiny image classifier yourself, in your browser, with your own webcam. This is the full machine learning loop — examples in, a working model out — in about ten minutes.

Watch an agent work, not just answer

Give an AI assistant with tool access a small multi-step task and actually read through what it did along the way, not just the final result. That's the difference between a chat reply and an agent.

Read one plain-language explainer

Skip the research paper. The labs building these systems — Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind — all publish plain-English explainers on their own sites about what their models can and can't actually do.
