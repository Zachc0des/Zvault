01Development timeline

Two centuries, one element

Silicon was isolated as a curiosity of 19th-century chemistry. It took over a century of parallel work in metallurgy, radio engineering, and materials science before it became the substrate of computing.

1824

First isolation

Jöns Jacob Berzelius purifies silicon from potassium fluorosilicate, identifying it as a distinct element.

Stockholm, Sweden
1906

The "cat's whisker" detector

Greenleaf Whittier Pickard patents a silicon crystal radio detector — silicon's first job as a semiconductor, decades before the word existed.

Massachusetts, USA
1947

The transistor — but germanium first

Bardeen, Brattain, and Shockley build the first working transistor at Bell Labs. It uses germanium, silicon's chemical cousin; silicon was still too hard to purify.

New Jersey, USA
1954

The first silicon transistor

Gordon Teal at Texas Instruments grows single-crystal silicon and builds the first practical silicon transistor, prizing its heat tolerance over germanium.

Texas, USA
1955–56

Crystal growing goes industrial

The Czochralski method — originally developed for metals in Poland in 1916 — is adapted to pull large, defect-free silicon crystals into cylindrical ingots.

Adapted in the USA, method rooted in Poland
1958–59

The integrated circuit

Jack Kilby (Texas Instruments) demonstrates the first integrated circuit; Robert Noyce (Fairchild) independently files a practical silicon-based design months later.

Texas & California, USA
1968

Intel is founded

Noyce and Gordon Moore leave Fairchild to found Intel in Santa Clara, anchoring what will become Silicon Valley's semiconductor core.

Santa Clara, California, USA
1971

First commercial microprocessor

The Intel 4004 packs an entire CPU onto one silicon chip, built with roughly 10-micron process technology.

California, USA
1980s

Japan's memory era

NEC, Toshiba, and Hitachi come to dominate global DRAM production, briefly overtaking US manufacturers on yield and cost.

Japan
1987

The foundry model is born

Morris Chang founds TSMC on a new idea: a fab that only manufactures chips designed by others. It reshapes the entire industry's geography.

Hsinchu, Taiwan
1990s–2000s

Korea scales memory

Samsung and SK Hynix invest heavily to lead global DRAM and NAND flash production.

South Korea
2010s

Extreme ultraviolet lithography

ASML commercializes EUV machines, the only tools in the world capable of patterning the smallest modern transistor features.

Veldhoven, Netherlands
2020s

Silicon as strategic asset

Export controls, the US CHIPS Act, and EU/Japan/India subsidy programs turn silicon manufacturing into a matter of national policy, as fabs push past 3-nanometer nodes.

Global — USA, Taiwan, EU, Japan, India

02The creation process

From beach to chip: nine orders of purity

Ordinary sand is roughly 99% silica. A processor chip needs silicon purified to 99.9999999% — "nine nines" — then grown into a flawless single crystal before it can hold a transistor.

Quartz mining

High-purity quartzite and silica sand are quarried — not ordinary beach sand, but specific deposits low in metal impurities.

~99% SiO₂

Carbothermic reduction

Quartz is smelted with carbon in an electric arc furnace at around 2,000°C, yielding metallurgical-grade silicon.

~98–99% pure

Siemens purification

Silicon is converted to trichlorosilane gas, distilled, and deposited back onto a heated rod, stripping out nearly all remaining impurities.

"Electronic grade" — 9N to 11N pure

Czochralski crystal growth

A seed crystal is dipped into molten silicon and slowly withdrawn while rotating, pulling up a single flawless cylindrical ingot several feet long.

Single-crystal boule

Wafer slicing & polishing

A diamond wire saw slices the ingot into wafers thinner than a human hair, which are then lapped and polished to a mirror finish.

300mm standard wafers

Photolithography & doping

Layer by layer, UV or EUV light etches circuit patterns while ion implantation introduces boron or phosphorus to create transistor junctions.

Features as small as 2–3nm

Dicing, bonding & packaging

The finished wafer is cut into individual dies, wired to a package with gold or copper leads, and sealed for the outside world.

Ready-to-ship chip

04Adoption & culture

How an element became a place, an era, a value chain

Silicon's biography isn't just technical — it reshaped language, geography, and geopolitics in its own image.

Naming a place

"Silicon Valley," 1971

Journalist Don Hoefler coined the term for California's Santa Clara Valley in a trade paper, naming a region after the material its companies manufactured — a naming convention later exported worldwide.

Exported branding

A template repeated globally

The "Silicon ___" formula spread far beyond California: Silicon Fen (Cambridge, UK), Silicon Wadi (Israel), Silicon Saxony (Dresden, Germany), and Silicon Glen (Scotland) each borrowed the name to signal a tech cluster, even where little silicon was physically made.

Consumer adoption

From lab curiosity to household object

Between the 1970s pocket calculator and the 2007 smartphone, silicon chips moved from specialist labs into nearly every home appliance, car, and pocket on Earth, compressing decades of adoption curves that once took a generation.

Geopolitical shift

The chip as a strategic resource

By the 2020s, silicon supply chains were treated with the seriousness once reserved for oil — export restrictions, national subsidy packages, and "friend-shoring" strategies now shape where fabs are built.

Cultural residue

A material that became a metaphor

"Silicon" now stands in for computing itself, much as "steel" once stood for industrial might — shorthand used in film, fiction, and political speech long after most people have never seen a wafer.
