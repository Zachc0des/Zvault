One light-day,
and everything
built to reach it

Two space stories are running at once and keep getting welded together in headlines. One is an enormous buildout: a million-satellite compute constellation, filed, funded and partly designed. The other is an enormous subtraction: a 1977 spacecraft losing four watts a year, 172 astronomical units out, being kept alive one instrument at a time. This is what each actually is right now — and what the first can and cannot do for the second.

Voyager 1 reaches one light-day — 18 Nov 2026, 02:16:07 PST
——

At that instant a command leaving Earth needs a full day to arrive, and a reply needs another. NASA has the moment down to the second: 16,094,799,096 miles.

The power ledger

Three radioisotope thermoelectric generators convert heat from decaying plutonium-238 into electricity. There is no refuelling, no recharging and no degraded-mode trick that adds watts back. Since the late 1990s the team has been retiring instruments and heaters in a pre-agreed order. The cosmic ray subsystem went in 2025. The Low-Energy Charged Particles experiment — running almost continuously since launch — went in April 2026. Voyager 1 is now down to a magnetometer and the plasma wave subsystem.

If I send a command and say “good morning, Voyager 1” at 8 a.m. on a Monday, I get the response back Wednesday at about 8 a.m. Suzy Dodd — Voyager project manager, NASA JPL

The ground segment is the other risk

Voyager's survival depends as much on Earth-side hardware as on its own. Goldstone's 70-metre DSS-14 has been out of service since a September 2025 accident and is not expected back until 2028. In late July 2026 a wildfire forced the evacuation of the Madrid Deep Space Communications Complex; operational control passed to Goldstone on 24 July and the antennas were off the air, though inspection on 25 July found damage confined to pavement and vegetation with no significant structural harm to antennas or buildings. Voyager 1 is the most demanding customer on the network — faint enough that contacts benefit from arraying multiple dishes at one site. A NASA Inspector General audit had already found the Deep Space Network oversubscribed by as much as 40% at peak, with the replacement-antenna program behind schedule and over budget.

Both stories are, underneath, a materials problem. Voyager has survived 49 years of vacuum, thermal cycling and radiation on 1970s material science. What actually kills things out there

Vacuum

Anything volatile leaves — moisture, plasticizers, solvents. It condenses on optics and radiators, which is why outgassing limits, not strength, disqualify most consumer materials. Clean metal surfaces pressed together can also cold-weld.

Thermal cycling

Roughly −270 °C to +430 °C depending on orbit and orientation, crossed repeatedly. Mismatched expansion coefficients between bonded materials do the damage, not the extremes themselves.

Radiation

Trapped particles, cosmic rays and UV. Polymers lose bonds; semiconductors take single-event upsets. Google's accelerator testing of a five-year LEO dose on TPUs is exactly this problem, run in a lab.

Micrometeoroids and debris

Millimetre particles at kilometres per second. Why AI1's radiator is described with shielding — a pumped fluid loop punctured in orbit is not repairable.

A fair question, and one the original version of this file answered sloppily: if all we have is a radio signal, how do we know it comes from where NASA says it does? The honest answer is that the signal is the least of the evidence. Several independent physical constraints have to agree, and they are checked by parties who do not work for NASA.

Geometry you cannot forge

Three complexes — Goldstone, Madrid, Canberra — sit roughly 120° apart in longitude. Which one can see a given spacecraft is fixed by Earth's rotation and the target's sky position. Handovers happen on that schedule, publicly, on NASA's live DSN feed. Voyager 2's declination is far enough south that only Canberra's DSS-43 can command it at all — a constraint nobody would invent, and one that has caused real operational pain.

Doppler that tracks two orbits

The received frequency shifts with the relative velocity of transmitter and receiver. That includes Earth's own ~30 km/s orbital motion, which imprints an annual sinusoid on top of Voyager's steady recession. Reproducing that would mean faking a signature that encodes both bodies' trajectories, continuously, for decades.

Reception outside the agency

In late 2024, after a fault pushed Voyager 1 onto an S-band transmitter unused since 1981, volunteers at the Dwingeloo 25-metre telescope in the Netherlands — CAMRAS, with a purpose-mounted feed — detected the carrier, having corrected for predicted Doppler; the measured shift matched. The same group picked up Voyager 1 in 2006. This is not backyard equipment, and the earlier version of this briefing was wrong to say so. It is a handful of large dishes worldwide, operated independently of NASA.

A link budget that decays on schedule

23 watts through a 3.7 m dish, arriving on the order of 10⁻¹⁶ watts — a modern wristwatch radiates billions of times more power than what reaches the antenna. Received strength has followed an inverse-square curve for 49 years. Convolutional and Reed–Solomon coding flags corrupted frames rather than inventing plausible ones.

Two spacecraft, two paths, one boundary

Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause in August 2012; Voyager 2 crossed in November 2018 on a completely different trajectory, and measured a boundary with the same physics and different details. Independent confirmation of an unfalsifiable-in-advance prediction is the strongest item on this list.

Delay that only grows

Round-trip time is set by c and distance, and nothing at either end can shorten it. Voyager 1's one-way light time lengthens by roughly five seconds a day. Every command sequence for 49 years has been scheduled against that number, and the responses have arrived in the predicted windows.

Two claims removed from the previous version

Struck

“Amateur radio enthusiasts detect Voyager with backyard equipment.” Overstated. Detection requires a large, well-characterized dish and careful Doppler correction. The accurate version is above, and it is a better argument anyway.

Struck

“Voyager data predicts cosmic events before other instruments observe them.” Not a real claim. Voyager measures the local interstellar medium in situ; that is valuable precisely because it cannot be observed remotely, but it does not forecast events elsewhere.

Where skepticism belongs

Not on whether the link is real — that is over-determined. Put it on the specific numbers instead: distances published on different dates disagree because Earth moves, remaining-power figures are estimates rather than telemetry, and end-of-mission dates have slipped repeatedly in both directions.

Orbital data centers do not upgrade Voyager. There is no servicing mission, none is planned, and anything launched today would arrive long after the RTGs are exhausted. The previous version of this file said that and then spent four panels quietly walking it back. Here is the tighter version.

What genuinely transfers

  • Radiation-tolerant commodity silicon. This is the real spillover. Google put Trillium TPUs in a particle accelerator and they survived a five-year LEO dose. Starcloud is designing a space-rated chip for around 2028. If commercial pressure makes rad-tolerant parts cheap and current, future probes carry vastly more onboard autonomy inside the same power envelope Voyager was stuck with in 1977 — which matters enormously when the round trip is two days and the craft has to decide for itself.
  • Optical terminals at production volume. Deep-space laser communication already works; NASA's optical demonstrations proved it beyond the Moon. What it lacks is cheap, mass-produced, flight-proven terminals. A constellation that builds hundreds of thousands of laser links makes that hardware ordinary.
  • Relay-first architecture. If a maintained backbone exists, future probes can shed the giant high-gain antenna Voyager carries and spend the mass on instruments — at the cost of depending on infrastructure someone has to keep alive for decades.

What does not

  • Signal processing does not need to be in orbit. Better extraction from a weak downlink is a receiver-side algorithm problem. It runs fine on the ground, on a machine you can service. Putting the GPU in LEO adds latency and a launch cost and buys nothing here.
  • DSN relief is indirect at best. Commercial constellations do not use the Deep Space Network, so they were never the congestion. The near-term threats to Voyager's downlink time are concrete and terrestrial: DSS-14 out until 2028, Madrid interrupted by fire, a replacement-antenna program running late and over budget.
  • Nothing touches the light-day. No mesh, no compute node, no laser changes c.

Bottom line

The buildout will not extend Voyager's life or restore a single instrument — that ceiling is physics and 1977 hardware. What it plausibly does is make the components of the next interstellar mission cheap, current and abundant. Voyager's successor benefits. Voyager does not.

Materials

Reversed

Wood. Listed as “cannot survive.” LignoSat, a magnolia-wood CubeSat from Kyoto University and Sumitomo Forestry, was deployed from the ISS in December 2024 and held together structurally — there is no moisture in vacuum to rot it. Its communications failed, which is a different problem, and LignoSat-2 is in work.

Reclassified

Lead. Filed under “cannot sustain space travel,” which is wrong — lead survives fine. It loses on mass per unit of shielding. It now has its own verdict category.

Qualified

Concrete. Ordinary Portland concrete fails on bound water, but waterless formulations using sulfur or processed regolith are an active research line for surface construction.
